\begin{abstract}[figures/fig01_variance_components.pdf]
Wearable activity-recognition systems are compared by macro-F1 on people unseen in training, but how much of that
figure reflects the network rather than the people tested is unknown. We decompose the variance of stored runs of one
person-disjoint protocol (WHAR Arena v1) with a linear mixed model and a pre-registered test. On \val{H1.datasets}
held-out datasets (\val{H1.runs} runs of four networks, three seeds), the test-subject group accounts for
\val{H1.pi_P}\,\% of the within-dataset variance, \val{H1.R} times (97.5\,\% interval \val{H1.R_lo}--\val{H1.R_hi})
all network-related variance; the pre-registered criterion was met. The ordering holds with a ceiling-free metric and
on development datasets, where that metric leaves it unresolved, and it reverses across 17 heterogeneous published
architectures. For comparable networks, reported accuracy depends more on who is tested than on the network:
comparisons must be paired by split and report the spread across subjects.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{W}{earable} inertial sensing systems for human activity recognition (HAR) are designed and chosen on one number:
macro-F1 on subjects held out from training~\cite{bulling2014tutorial}. Evaluating on unseen people removes the
optimism of random cross-validation~\cite{hammerla2015lets}, and how people are partitioned between training and test
data changes the reported accuracy~\cite{lockhart2014limitations}. People move differently~\cite{qian2021latent} and
devices sense differently~\cite{stisen2015smart}, so a test set of a few subjects carries its own variation. WHAR
Arena~\cite{burzer2026arena} fixed one person-disjoint protocol across 30 datasets and found the state of the art
distributed over architectures rather than held by one; it reports one training run per split, so it cannot separate
the effect of who is tested from the training seed. Machine-learning benchmarks are known to depend on such sources of
variation~\cite{bouthillier2021accounting}, but their sizes in wearable HAR are unknown.

This letter measures them. Under one fixed protocol it decomposes the variance of macro-F1 into the group of test
subjects, the network, their interactions and the training seed; it tests the ordering of these terms with a
pre-registered analysis on held-out datasets; it checks the result with a ceiling-free metric, development data and
the benchmark's independent architectures; and it reports, as exploratory, which design choices and which sensing
properties of a dataset go with large accuracy differences, what the networks' temporal memories learned, and how far
the test subjects lie from the training subjects in the networks' embedding space.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Datasets and sensors}
The 18 datasets of the stored runs (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}, Fig.~\ref{fig:sensors}) were recorded with smartphones,
body-worn inertial nodes, a smartwatch, smart glasses and a helmet. Every dataset has accelerometers; most add

\subsection{Protocol and stored runs}
All runs follow WHAR Arena v1 with its paper-era code~\cite{burzer2026arena,burzer2025whar}: 3-s windows with 50\,\%
overlap at the native sampling rate; $k=10$ subject groups, each split testing on one group
(\val{subjects_per_split.min} to \val{subjects_per_split.max} subjects) that never appears in training or validation;
validation from 20\,\% of the training subjects' windows; a z-score fitted on the training split; macro-F1 over all
classes, with classes absent from a test split counted as 0. Apart from a label-permutation control, no network was
trained for this letter: we analyse stored runs and their checkpoints. Three sets enter (Table~\ref{tab:vc}): S1, ten networks that share one skeleton and differ
in single components (with and without temporal memory, e.g.\ S4D~\cite{gu2022efficiently,gu2022parameterization} and
Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba}, or window statistics), on ten development datasets with three seeds; S2, four of these
networks on eight datasets never used for this question, with three seeds; S3, the benchmark's 17 published models
(deep networks such as DeepConvLSTM~\cite{ordonez2016deep,hammerla2016deep}, and Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001random},
SVM and k-NN on tsfresh features~\cite{christ2018tsfresh}) re-run with the same code on four datasets, one run per
split. A pre-registered inclusion rule removed datasets whose stored runs disagree on split sizes, because their split
indices do not name the same subjects across runs (one dataset each in S1 and S2). Fig.~\ref{fig:design} summarises
the design, the runs analysed and how subjects are assigned to test groups.

\subsection{Networks}
The ten networks of S1 and S2 share one skeleton (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, Table~\ref{tab:models}). A linear encoder maps
the $C$ channels of each sample to $D$ features, $N$ residual blocks process the sequence, and the features are
averaged over the window, joined in four networks by a window-level side path, normalised (LayerNorm) and classified by
a linear head. The vector the head receives is the network's embedding (Section~\ref{sec:emb}). In a residual block, a
LayerNorm and a linear map give per-channel signals $u$ that feed a memory; $u$ and the memory's outputs are
concatenated, mixed linearly and passed through a GELU into the residual sum. RecurrenceNet's memory keeps soft rows of
a recurrence plot~\cite{eckmann1987recurrence} over ten lags: whether a learned two-dimensional projection of the
signal is back where it was $\tau$ samples earlier. HysteresisNet keeps four play operators per channel, whose output
moves only when the input leaves a band of half-width $r$ around it~\cite{brokate1996hysteresis} (here with
input-dependent widths and a clock channel). QuantileNet keeps four online quantile trackers per channel, stochastic
approximation steps~\cite{robbins1951stochastic} that settle on a running quantile of level $\tau$. The no-memory
control sets every play width to zero, which makes each operator the identity, and the two networks without memory
use plain blocks. S4D~\cite{gu2022parameterization} and Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba} use their own blocks, a diagonal
state-space convolution and a selective scan. The side paths read per-channel window statistics (mean, standard
deviation, minimum and maximum, the kind of features behind the Random Forest) or invariants of each three-axis
sensor's temporal operator and of the couplings between sensors (``laws''). With the reference input of gate G1 (that
of UTD-MHAD), the networks have \val{models.params_ref_min} to \val{models.params_ref_max} parameters, and on the
datasets of S1 and S2 \val{models.params_all_min} to \val{models.params_all_max}.

\subsection{Variance model and pre-registered test}
For run $(m,d,s,r)$ of network $m$ on split $s$ of dataset $d$ with seed $r$,
\begin{equation}
y_{mdsr} = \mu_d + P_{ds} + M_m + (MD)_{md} + (MP)_{mds} + e_{mdsr},
\end{equation}
with $\mu_d$ fixed and the other terms independent zero-mean random effects, fitted by REML~\cite{patterson1971recovery}
in statsmodels~\cite{seabold2010statsmodels}. The estimand is $R=\sigma^2_P/(\sigma^2_M+\sigma^2_{MD}+\sigma^2_{MP})$,
the variance of the test-subject group over all network-related variance; $\pi_P$ is $\sigma^2_P$'s share of the
within-dataset variance. Intervals are percentile 97.5\,\% intervals from 1000 cluster-bootstrap resamples that draw
splits within datasets~\cite{field2007bootstrapping}. Before any variance analysis of S2, we registered H1 ($R>1$ on
S2), with success if the interval's lower bound exceeds 1 and failure if $R\le 1$, sealed the analysis code by hash,
and logged S2's single read. S2's per-dataset means were known from an earlier study; its variance components were
not. For S3 (one run per split) the interaction and the seed cannot be separated, so the conservative
$R'=\sigma^2_P/(\sigma^2_M+\sigma^2_{MD}+\sigma^2_e)$ is reported.

\subsection{Exploratory analyses}
Component contrasts on S1 pair two networks by split, average the seeds, and summarise over datasets with a
$t$-interval and a Wilcoxon test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual,demsar2006statistical}. Over the benchmark's 30 datasets
we regress the published single-run results on standardised dataset properties (ordinary least squares); these
associations are correlational, and their $p$-values are not corrected for multiplicity. Within each dataset of S1 and
S2 we compare the spread of the split means (over networks and seeds) with that of the network means (over splits and
seeds). From the stored checkpoints of eight S1 datasets we read what the memories learned and compare it with the
same networks freshly initialised: S4D's mode time constants and decay rates, the play widths, the quantile levels
and steps, and the recurrence radii. None of these analyses was pre-registered.

\subsection{Embedding analysis}\label{sec:emb}
This analysis is exploratory and uses inference only. For the seed-0 checkpoint of every split of the
\val{emb.datasets} datasets whose caches on our cluster reproduce the stored runs (eight of S1 with the ten networks,
three of S2 with four), the harness rebuilds the split and the network embeds every training and test window; the
regenerated macro-F1 equals the stored one in \val{emb.regenerated_exact} of \val{emb.regenerated_cases} cases (largest
difference \val{emb.max_abs_regenerated_minus_stored_macro_f1} points). Three quantities describe each network and
split. First, the class-conditional shift of the test group~\cite{zhang2013domain}: for every class present in both,
the distance between the test and the training class means, in units of the training class's root-mean-square spread
per dimension, averaged over classes. The same shift is computed in a sensor-feature space that no network shapes
(per-channel mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum of the normalised window). Second, the subject information
in the training embeddings: the balanced accuracy of a linear probe~\cite{alain2016understanding} (logistic
regression) that predicts the training subject, in five folds grouped by recording session so that overlapping
windows never straddle folds, on at most \val{emb.probe_windows} windows, against chance (one over the number of
training subjects). Third, the same probe for the activity. Within each dataset we rank-correlate the test group's
shift, averaged over networks, with the split's macro-F1 (Spearman), and we pool the within-dataset ranks over
datasets with more than three splits. One case, fixed before the analysis (UCI-HAR, S4D, split~0), is mapped to two
dimensions with t-SNE~\cite{vandermaaten2008visualizing}.

\subsection{Verification}
For verification, the
benchmark's Random Forest, re-run with our code, reproduces its published macro-F1 on four datasets (e.g.\
\val{g3.rf.uci_har.rerun} against \val{g3.rf.uci_har.figure6} on UCI-HAR); test predictions were regenerated from
\val{g5.reexecuted} checkpoints, \val{g5.exact} of which reproduced the stored metrics exactly and all within two
test windows; an independent REML implementation and negative controls (e.g.\ permuting network labels within cells)
confirmed the analysis; and S4D retrained on permuted training labels (\val{ls.runs} runs on UP-Fall and UCA-EHAR)
fell to chance, with a test macro-F1 of \val{ls.mean_shuffled_test_macro_f1} against \val{ls.mean_chance_test_macro_f1}
by chance and \val{ls.mean_stored_test_macro_f1} with the true labels. Of the \val{H1.runs} confirmatory runs, the gate
regenerated \val{g5.s2} on the caches the stored runs used, within that tolerance. Beyond the gate, every analysed run
with a checkpoint was re-executed (\val{repro.analysed.reexecuted} of \val{repro.analysed.runs}):
\val{repro.analysed.exact} reproduce their records exactly, \val{repro.analysed.within_tolerance} within two test
windows, and none differs by more than \val{repro.analysed.max_abs_delta_macro_f1} F1 points. The residual
differences have one source. Several parsers number activities and subjects, and order recordings, in the order the
file system lists them, and many stored runs were built on cloud machines whose listing order cannot be recreated.
For DSADS we recovered that order from the checkpoints themselves, since each network is near-perfect on its own
training subjects, which identifies its output labels and its test subjects, and rebuilt the cache accordingly; for
USC-HAD and three datasets whose caches we rebuilt, the test sets are identical and only the draw of validation
windows, and with it the train-only z-score, differs. The \val{repro.retrained.runs} runs without a checkpoint were retrained to obtain one;
\val{repro.retrained.exact} of them reproduce their record's macro-F1 exactly, and the stored records remain the
ones analysed.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Confirmatory test}
On S2 the test-subject group dominates (Fig.~\ref{fig:vc}, Table~\ref{tab:vc}): $\sigma^2_P=\val{H1.s2_P}$
(F1 points$^2$) against $\sigma^2_M=\val{H1.s2_M}$, $\sigma^2_{MD}=\val{H1.s2_MD}$ and $\sigma^2_{MP}=\val{H1.s2_MP}$,
with the seed at $\sigma^2_e=\val{H1.s2_e}$. Hence $R=\val{H1.R}$ [\val{H1.R_lo}, \val{H1.R_hi}] and
$\pi_P=\val{H1.pi_P}$\,\% [\val{H1.pi_P_lo}, \val{H1.pi_P_hi}]: the pre-registered decision is \emph{\val{H1.decision}},
and the secondary criterion ($\pi_P$ lower bound $\ge 40$\,\%) is met.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}
Balanced accuracy, which averages recall over the classes present and so has no per-split ceiling, gives
$R=\val{sens1_S2_balanced_accuracy.R}$ [\val{sens1_S2_balanced_accuracy.R_lo}, \val{sens1_S2_balanced_accuracy.R_hi}]
on S2: ceilings enlarge the subject share but do not create it. On the development set S1 the ordering is the same
with macro-F1 ($R=\val{sens2_S1_macro_f1.R}$ [\val{sens2_S1_macro_f1.R_lo}, \val{sens2_S1_macro_f1.R_hi}]), also
without its two-subject dataset ActRecTut ($R=\val{sens3_S1_without_actrectut.R}$); with balanced accuracy the point
estimate stays above 1 but the interval does not exclude it ($R=\val{sens2_S1_balanced_accuracy.R}$
[\val{sens2_S1_balanced_accuracy.R_lo}, \val{sens2_S1_balanced_accuracy.R_hi}]). Across the benchmark's 17 published architectures
it reverses: $R'=\val{sens4_S3_macro_f1.R}$ [\val{sens4_S3_macro_f1.R_lo}, \val{sens4_S3_macro_f1.R_hi}], with
$\sigma^2_M=\val{sens4_S3_macro_f1.s2_M}$ against $\sigma^2_P=\val{sens4_S3_macro_f1.s2_P}$. These architectures range
from k-NN to attention networks; their differences are far larger than those between the shared-skeleton networks.

subjects. On UCI-HAR the split means have a standard deviation of \val{spread.uci_har.sd_split_means} F1 points against

\val{spread.up_fall.sd_model_means}. In the network $\times$ split cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:spread}b, c) the columns
dominate: a test group that is hard for one network is hard for the others.

\subsection{Validation on the training subjects}
Each run's validation windows come from its training subjects, so its validation score measures accuracy on people
the network has seen (Fig.~\ref{fig:val}). On the \val{val.datasets} datasets whose runs all recorded it, the

\subsection{Exploratory findings}
Within S1, the one design choice worth several points is temporal memory (Fig.~\ref{fig:explore}a): S4D gains
$\val{contrast.s4d-hystnet_nomem.9.mean}$ [$\val{contrast.s4d-hystnet_nomem.9.lo}$,
$\val{contrast.s4d-hystnet_nomem.9.hi}$] points over the same network without memory
(\val{contrast.s4d-hystnet_nomem.9.wins} of 9 datasets), while the type of memory moves macro-F1 by at most a few
points (Mamba $-$ S4D: $\val{contrast.mamba-s4d.9.mean}$ [$\val{contrast.mamba-s4d.9.lo}$,
$\val{contrast.mamba-s4d.9.hi}$]). Over the 30 datasets, a larger channel count goes with a larger margin of the

training subjects go with a larger gap between the best and the median deep network
($\val{ols.gap.subjects.beta}$ per standard deviation, $p\val{ols.gap.subjects.p}$). No property predicts how hard a
dataset is ($R^2=\val{ols.level.r2}$).

\subsection{What the memories learned}
The memories' own parameters barely move in training (Fig.~\ref{fig:memory}; \val{learned.checkpoints} checkpoints of
eight S1 datasets, each against the same network freshly initialised). The quantile levels change by a median of
\val{learned.quantilenet_tau.change_p50} (95th percentile \val{learned.quantilenet_tau.change_p95}), the recurrence
radii by \val{learned.recurrencenet.change_p50} (\val{learned.recurrencenet.change_p95}), and S4D's decay rates by a
median of \val{learned.s4d_a_real.change_p50} in $\log_{10}$. The play widths (median
\val{learned.hystnet_sel_clock.p50}, initially \val{learned.hystnet_sel_clock.init_p50}) and S4D's mode time constants
(median \val{learned.s4d.p50} window lengths, initially \val{learned.s4d.init_p50}; \val{learned.s4d.above_window}\,\%

The time and amplitude scales at which each memory works are thus fixed by its design and initialisation; whatever
memory adds (Fig.~\ref{fig:explore}a), it adds at the scales it was given.

\subsection{Embeddings}
In the networks' embedding space, the hard test groups are the distant ones (Fig.~\ref{fig:emb}). Within datasets,
the splits whose test subjects lie further from the training subjects, class by class, are the splits on which the
networks score lower: the pooled within-dataset rank correlation is
$\rho=\val{emb.pooled_within_dataset_rank_rho_embedding_ccs_f1}$ ($p\val{emb.pooled_within_dataset_rank_p_embedding}$),

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Here ``comparable'' networks share one design and differ in single components, so that their dataset means differ
by a few points. For such networks, who is tested decides more of a reported macro-F1 than which network is used.
The variance between test-subject groups governs the absolute level, and with it every comparison made across
studies, splits or subject groups. A comparison paired by split cancels it, but is then limited by the network
$\times$ split interaction and the seed, which on S2 are as large as the network terms themselves (Table~\ref{tab:vc}).
For a wearable sensing study this gives three rules. Record enough subjects to estimate the spread across test-subject
groups, and report it. Compare networks paired on the same splits, never by means over different subjects. Average
several training seeds. The reversal on S3 bounds the claim: a weak baseline is told apart from a strong network easily,
and the subject effect matters when choosing between designs that are already competitive. The exploratory
associations point at the sensing system: with many channels, hand-crafted features stay competitive, and with few
recorded subjects the choice of network matters more.

The exploratory analyses agree with the decomposition and say where the subject effect sits. Within datasets, test
groups spread accuracy more than networks do (Fig.~\ref{fig:spread}). Validation on the training subjects overstates the
person-disjoint score, on some datasets by tens of points, and ranks the networks differently (Fig.~\ref{fig:val}), so
early stopping and model selection should hold out whole subjects. A test group is hard when its windows, class by
class, lie far from the training subjects' in the space the classifier reads, a space that still identifies the
training subjects (Fig.~\ref{fig:emb}), whereas the temporal memories keep the scales they start with
(Fig.~\ref{fig:memory}). Together these suggest that, for comparable networks, the training subjects and how
representations align them are more promising levers for accuracy on new people than another temporal operator.

The analysis covers one protocol, and three seeds per run. The S1 and S2 networks share one skeleton, so their
components are controlled but not independent designs. S2's means were known before the registration. The 30-dataset
results re-analyse published single runs and are correlational, and subjects and channels co-vary across datasets.
The embedding analysis uses the seed-0 checkpoints of eleven datasets, and the validation analysis only the datasets
whose runs recorded a validation score. The intervals resample test-subject groups and treat the networks as fixed: they say how precisely $R$ is known for
these networks, not for networks in general, and S3 shows that another population of networks can reverse it.

\section{Conclusion}
Under a fixed person-disjoint protocol, who is tested accounts for most of the variance of macro-F1 among comparable
wearable HAR networks, as a pre-registered test on held-out datasets confirmed; across heterogeneous architectures
the network dominates. Studies of wearable sensing systems should size and report their test subjects accordingly.
